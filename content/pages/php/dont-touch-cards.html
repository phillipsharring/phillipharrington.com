Don't Touch Cards Phillip Harrington

Every lesson so far has been about handling dangerous things carefully. This one is about not handling them at all. If your site takes card payments, the safest card number is one that never reaches your server. Not checked carefully, not encrypted, not deleted quickly: never there. A payment company's page takes the card, and your code only ever hears whether it worked.

What's PCI DSS?

The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard: the rules for anyone who stores, processes or transmits card numbers. It's set by the PCI Security Standards Council, which the big card brands (Visa, Mastercard, American Express, Discover and JCB) formed in 2006. Your bank or payment company makes following it a condition of taking cards.

The full standard is twelve broad requirements broken into hundreds of detailed ones: networks, firewalls, encryption, access control, logging, scanning, testing, policies and paperwork, and an assessment every year. It applies to every system that touches a card number, and every system connected to those.

How Much of It Is Yours

Small merchants prove they comply with a Self-Assessment Questionnaire, and which one depends on how close cards come to you:

  • SAQ A: you've handed everything to a provider. The visitor types their card on the provider's page, or in the provider's frame, and your systems never store, process or send a card number. The shortest one.
  • SAQ A-EP: the card goes straight to the provider, but your own page builds the form or loads the scripts that collect it, so your server's security affects it. Much longer.
  • SAQ D: card numbers pass through your server. Nearly the whole standard, for every system involved.
    • What's a Hosted Checkout?

    A payment page that the payment company runs. Your server asks it to start a payment (this product, this price), it answers with an address, and you send the visitor there. They type the card on its page; it sends them back to yours when they're done. Stripe Checkout is one. PayPal, Square, Adyen and others have their own.

    How It Works

    With Stripe Checkout:

    1. The visitor presses Buy. Your page sends a POST to your server; no card fields.
    2. Your server asks Stripe for a Checkout Session, with the product and price from your own code, and where to send the visitor afterward.
    3. Stripe answers with the session's address, on checkout.stripe.com.
    4. Your server redirects the visitor there, with a 303.
    5. The visitor types their card on Stripe's page. Stripe charges it.
    6. Stripe sends the visitor back to your success page, and separately tells your server with a webhook: a signed request saying the payment was completed.
    7. Your server checks, with Stripe, that the payment really happened, and only then ships the mug.

    The card number went from the visitor's keyboard to Stripe. It was never in your request, your code, your logs, your database or your backups. That puts you in SAQ A, and puts the rest of the standard on Stripe, which is what you're paying them for.

    In January 2009, Heartland Payment Systems, a company that processed card payments for hundreds of thousands of businesses, announced that attackers had been inside its network for months, recording card numbers as they passed through. About 130 million cards were exposed. The way in, according to the prosecutors who charged Albert Gonzalez for it, was SQL injection. Try It

    In PHP
  • new Stripe\StripeClient(['api_key' => $key]), with the key from the environment. Test keys start sk_test_, live ones sk_live_.
  • $stripe->checkout->sessions->create([...]) with the mode, line items, success_url and cancel_url. Amounts are whole cents, an int, never a float.
  • header('Location: ' . $session->url, true, 303) to send the visitor.
  • $stripe->checkout->sessions->retrieve($id) and payment_status === 'paid' to find out what really happened.
  • Stripe\Webhook::constructEvent($body, $signature, $secret) to check a webhook's signature before believing it. Taking a Payment with Stripe Checkout builds the whole thing.
    • Gotchas
  • The success page is not proof of payment. It's an address anyone can type. Never trust input: ship on a signed webhook, or after asking Stripe, as thanks.php does. Visiting it with a made-up session id gets "We haven't received your payment yet."
  • The price comes from your code. If checkout.php took the amount from the form, you'd be back in Never Trust Input's mug shop, selling at whatever price the visitor typed.
  • "We only keep the last four" is fine; "we keep it encrypted" isn't enough. Encrypted card numbers are still card numbers, in scope. And the card's security code, the extra three or four digits, may never be stored after the payment is approved, by anyone, even encrypted.
  • Card fields on your page are still on your page. Building your own form that sends cards straight to the provider with JavaScript keeps them off your server, but your page now controls the form, which is SAQ A-EP. A redirect or the provider's own embedded frame keeps you in SAQ A.
  • SAQ A is short, not nothing. You still fill it in, and your site still has to be secure, because a hacked site can send visitors to a fake checkout.
    • Break It

    You won't attack a card here. The danger of a card form on your own page isn't a clever payload; it's that card numbers start landing in places you never thought of as part of a payment system. Make public/naive.php, a shop with its own card form, and the debugging line every app has somewhere:

    With the shop running, buy a mug with Stripe's test card number:

    curl -d 'card_number=4242424242424242&card_expiry=12/34&card_cvc=123' http://localhost:8000/naive.php docker compose logs shop

    What's new:

    Print what a service has written to its output so far: here, the server's log. The service to read, as named in compose.yml. shop-1 | [Fri Nov 27 20:11:18 2026] New order: {"card_number":"4242424242424242","card_expiry":"12\/34","card_cvc":"123"}

    The card number, the expiry and the CVC, in your log. Nothing was charged, nothing was hacked, and you're already storing cardholder data, including the one part nobody is ever allowed to keep. In a real shop, that log is copied to a log service, kept for months, and backed up. Each of those is now a system that stores card numbers, and the whole of PCI DSS applies to all of them.

    And that's the honest version. A hacked server with a card form on it can do far worse: attackers who get in add a few lines that send each card to them as it's typed, and nobody notices until the cards are used.

    Fix It

    The fix is the shop from Try It: no card fields anywhere, and a redirect to Stripe. Delete naive.php, restart, buy through index.php, and check the log again:

    docker compose down docker compose up -d curl -X POST http://localhost:8000/checkout.php docker compose logs shop | grep -c 4242

    Only the new part:

    Count the matching lines instead of printing them. 0 means none.

    0. There's nothing to find, because there was never anything to log. No check, no encryption and no cleanup job: the card number never had a way in.

    Payments in one of my apps go through Stripe Checkout. No card number has ever touched its servers, and none can: there's no field for one to arrive in. Every question in this lesson about storing, encrypting or logging cards has the same answer there: there are none.

    Now and then, search your logs and database dumps for anything shaped like a card number: grep -E '\b[0-9]{13,19}\b' app.log. Most hits will be order numbers or timestamps. Any that aren't are a card number that got in somewhere it shouldn't, and better found by you.

    Cards stay off your server. What your server does send, every response, can carry instructions that make the browser itself part of your defense. Next: security headers.
