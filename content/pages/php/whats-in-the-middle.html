What's in the Middle Phillip Harrington

Client/Server drew two boxes and a line between them. Every Try It so far has been exactly that: your browser, straight to PHP on your own computer. On the real web, the line is a chain. A request passes through several servers before it reaches your code, and each one can answer it, change it, or stop it. Each is a server to whatever is in front of it, and a client to whatever is behind.

The Pieces A Reverse Proxy

The smallest middle there is: one server in front of your app that takes every request, passes it along, and passes the answer back. It's usually a web server, like nginx or Apache, sitting in front of PHP. It handles the jobs your code shouldn't have to: HTTPS, sending static files like images and stylesheets, and holding on to slow connections so your app doesn't.

A Load Balancer

For when one copy of your app isn't enough. It spreads requests across several app servers, checks which ones are healthy, and stops sending traffic to any that aren't. Visitors never know there's more than one.

A CDN

A content delivery network: servers all over the world, close to your visitors, that keep copies of your files and pages. DNS points your name at the CDN, so a visitor in Tokyo gets your stylesheet from a server in Tokyo, and your own server never hears about it. Only what the CDN doesn't have, or isn't allowed to keep, goes all the way back.

A Firewall

A web application firewall reads each request and blocks the ones that look like attacks or floods before they reach you. Useful, and never a reason to skip checking input in your own code: it catches the common attacks, not all of them.

In the early 2000s, a link from the tech news site Slashdot could send more visitors to a small site in an hour than it normally saw in a year, and the site would fall over. People called it being "slashdotted." Caching, and later CDNs, are a big part of why a sudden crowd mostly doesn't knock sites over anymore. How It Works

A busy site's request might go like this:

  1. The CDN gets it first. If it has a fresh copy of the answer, it sends that, and it's done.
  2. If not, a firewall checks it, and turns away anything that looks like an attack.
  3. A load balancer picks one of the app servers.
  4. That server's reverse proxy handles the HTTPS and the static files.
  5. What's left reaches PHP.

A small site might have only step four, or none at all. Either way, each layer can change what your code sees:

  • The address. PHP's REMOTE_ADDR is whoever connected to PHP, which is the last server in the chain, not the visitor. The middle reports the visitor's address in a header, X-Forwarded-For, instead.
  • The protocol. The visitor used HTTPS, but the last hop to PHP may be plain HTTP. That's the gotcha from the HTTP lesson.
  • The answer. A cache can send a copy without asking you, and a layer can rewrite your error pages, like the 403s in Request and Response.

    Directly, PHP sees your address, and no X-Forwarded-For. Through the proxy, PHP sees the proxy's address, and your address arrives in the header instead. Then add -I to the second one, and the proxy's mark is there in the answer: X-Passed-Through: proxy.

    Now lie to it:

    curl -H 'X-Forwarded-For: 1.2.3.4' http://localhost:8000

    New here:

    Add a header to the request: its name, a colon, and its value. Any header you like.

    PHP gets 1.2.3.4 first, then your real address. The proxy didn't replace what you sent; it added what it saw to the end. Everything before the last entry came from the client, which means from anyone. As you may recall, never trust input, and that includes headers. Trust only the entry your own proxy added: the last one, or the last few if you run several layers. Ctrl+C stops both.

    In PHP
  • $_SERVER['REMOTE_ADDR'] is the last hop: right when PHP faces visitors directly, wrong behind anything.
  • $_SERVER['HTTP_X_FORWARDED_FOR'] is the chain of addresses, visitor first. It's a header, so it's input: take the entries your own layers added, from the end, and ignore the rest.
  • $_SERVER['HTTP_X_FORWARDED_PROTO'], when your proxy sets it, says whether the visitor used HTTPS.
  • header('Cache-Control: private, no-store'); on any page that's about one person, so no layer in the middle keeps a copy.
    • Gotchas
  • Limits keyed on REMOTE_ADDR limit everyone. Behind a proxy, every request comes from the proxy's address, so "ten tries per address" becomes ten tries for the whole world.
  • The first X-Forwarded-For entry is whatever the visitor typed. Code that trusts it lets anyone pick their own address, and dodge any limit keyed on it.
  • A cache can show one person's page to another. A page that says "Hi, Alice" must not be cached anywhere shared. Mark it private.
  • The middle has its own errors. 502 Bad Gateway: a layer couldn't reach the one behind it. 504 Gateway Timeout: it gave up waiting, often after a minute or less, so a slow page can fail in the middle while PHP is still working on it.
    • One of my own apps checked its bot protection against the first address in X-Forwarded-For. That's the one a visitor writes, so anyone could have claimed any address they liked. The fix was one line: take the last one, the one the app's own CDN added.

    Most layers leave a mark in the response headers: a Server, a Via, a cache status, an Age (more than 0 means it came from a cache). When a change doesn't show up, curl -I the page and read them. You're often looking at a copy someone in the middle kept.
