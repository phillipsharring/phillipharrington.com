Security Headers Phillip Harrington

Every lesson in this section so far has been about your own code. This one is about the browser. Browsers will enforce a set of rules for your site, if you ask: run no scripts but yours, let no other site put your page in a frame, never treat a file as anything but what you said it was, never use plain HTTP again. You ask with response headers, a few lines sent with every page.

The Headers Content-Security-Policy

The big one. A list of where the page may load things from, by type:

Content-Security-Policy: default-src 'self'; script-src 'self'; object-src 'none'; base-uri 'self'; form-action 'self'; frame-ancestors 'none'
  • script-src 'self': scripts only from files on your own site. An inline <script> in the page, or an onclick attribute, is refused. So a script that an XSS hole lets in doesn't run.
  • default-src 'self': everything else, from your site only.
  • object-src 'none': no plugins.
  • base-uri and form-action: no changing where the page's relative links point, and forms only send to your own site.
  • frame-ancestors 'none': no site may show this page in a frame.
    •  X-Frame-Options

    The older way to say "don't frame me," DENY. frame-ancestors replaces it, but sending both costs nothing and covers old browsers.

    X-Content-Type-Options

    nosniff: use the Content-Type I sent, and don't guess. Without it, a browser can decide an uploaded "image" looks like HTML or script and treat it as such.

    Strict-Transport-Security

    HSTS: from now on, for this long, only ever reach this site over HTTPS. After one visit, the browser changes any http:// link to https:// itself, before anything is sent. See HTTP, HTTPS and TLS. Browsers ignore it when it arrives over plain HTTP.

    Referrer-Policy and Permissions-Policy

    Referrer-Policy limits what the Referer header tells other sites about the page a visitor came from: strict-origin-when-cross-origin sends only your site's address, not the full path. Permissions-Policy switches off browser features you never use, like the camera and microphone, so nothing on your page can ask for them.

    What's Clickjacking?

    Putting your page, invisible, in a frame on top of the attacker's page, lined up so that what the visitor clicks isn't what they see. They click "Click here to win!" and press your "Delete my account" button. It's your real page, logged in, CSRF token and all, so none of your other defenses notice.

    Jeremiah Grossman and Robert Hansen named clickjacking in 2008, and held back a talk about it until Adobe could fix the worst example: Flash Player's settings page could be framed invisibly, so a few innocent-looking clicks turned on someone's webcam and microphone. Microsoft added the X-Frame-Options header to Internet Explorer 8 the next year, and other browsers followed. How It Works
    1. Your page sends its headers with every response, before any of the HTML.
    2. The browser reads them and remembers the rules for this page, or this site.
    3. As it builds the page, it checks each script, frame, image and form against the rules, and refuses what isn't allowed, with a message in the developer tools' console.
    4. Another site trying to frame your page gets an empty frame or an error, never your page.

    Headers are a second line, not the first. A page with an XSS hole and a strict policy is still a page with an XSS hole; the policy just stops the most common way of using it. Fix the code, and send the headers too.

    In PHP
  • header('Name: value'), before any output. One function that sends them all, called at the top of every page, or once in a single point of entry.
  • header_remove('X-Powered-By'), or expose_php = Off in php.ini.
  • Or let the web server send them for every response, PHP or not. In nginx, add_header, with always.
  • Check them with curl -I, or the Network tab in the browser's developer tools.
    • Gotchas
  • A strict policy breaks inline scripts, including yours. onclick="...", <script> blocks in the page, and javascript: links all stop working. Move scripts into files. Adding 'unsafe-inline' to make the errors go away also lets every injected script back in.
  • HSTS is hard to take back. A browser that has seen a year-long max-age will refuse plain HTTP for a year. With includeSubDomains, that's every subdomain too. Start with a short max-age, and raise it once you're sure. Adding your site to the browsers' preload list is harder still to undo.
  • nginx forgets inherited headers. An add_header inside a location block means none of the server-level add_header lines apply there. And without always, they're not sent on error pages.
  • Headers go on every response, files included. nosniff matters most on uploads and downloads, like the images from File Uploads.
  • Skip X-XSS-Protection. It controlled a filter browsers have since removed. Old checklists still list it.

    The attacker's page: a big gold "Click here to win!" with your account page in a frame on top of it, lined up so the delete button sits right over the prize. Make evil.html:

    Open http://localhost:8000/evil.html. You'll see the faint outline of "Delete my account" over "Click here to win!" At opacity 0, you'd see only the prize. Click it: the frame says "Your account has been deleted." The click went to your real page, which had your session and a valid CSRF token, so it worked.

    (In a real attack this page is on another site. There, a session cookie with SameSite=Lax, from Sessions and Cookie Flags, isn't sent to the frame at all, which helps. But not every page needs a login to do damage, and a page on your own site, or a sibling subdomain, gets the cookie anyway.)

    Fix It

    The fixed page already sends frame-ancestors 'none' and X-Frame-Options: DENY. Make evil-fixed.html, the same page framing / instead of /naive.php:

    <iframe src="/"></iframe>

    Open http://localhost:8000/evil-fixed.html. There's the gold prize, and nothing over it: the browser refused to put your page in the frame. Click the prize, and nothing happens to your account. The console says the page was blocked by its frame-ancestors directive.

    Send your new policy as Content-Security-Policy-Report-Only first. The browser enforces nothing, but logs every violation in the console, so you can click through your whole site and see what would break: the inline script you forgot, the font from another site. Fix those, then rename the header.
