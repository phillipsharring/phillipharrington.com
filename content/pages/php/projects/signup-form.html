Signup Form Phillip Harrington

Something Database-y ended with a stolen table of password hashes. Sooner or later, somebody's table gets stolen; the question is what the thief can do with it. If the hashes were made the right way, not much. If they were made the way a lot of tutorials still show, the thief has the passwords, and because people reuse them, their email and their bank too.

You'll build a signup form that stores passwords the naive way, crack them in a blink, then fix it with the one function PHP gives you for this, and the few checks a signup form actually needs.

What You'll Use
  • Passwords: password_hash() and Nothing Else: the lesson this project acts out.
  • Inserting Data and Indexes: a UNIQUE index as the final word on duplicates.
  • Text and UTF-8: characters versus bytes, again.
  • Something Database-y: the same setup, and prepared statements.

    Break It

    Be the thief. Look at the table:

    docker compose exec db mysql -uapp -papp-password app -e 'SELECT email, password_hash FROM users' alice@example.com 482c811da5d5b4bc6d497ffa98491e38 bob@example.com 482c811da5d5b4bc6d497ffa98491e38

    The first problem is right there: the same password makes the same hash, so you can see that Alice and Bob share one, and crack them both at once. The second is that nobody needs to turn an md5 back into a password. They hash guesses and compare. crack.php does that with a short list of common passwords:

    docker compose exec php php crack.php 482c811da5d5b4bc6d497ffa98491e38

    "Found it: password123." Instantly. The list doesn't need to be clever, because md5 is fast, and fast is exactly wrong for this. speed.php counts how many guesses one second buys:

    docker compose exec php php speed.php md5: 6,058,101 guesses a second password_hash: 6 guesses a second

    Your numbers will differ; the gap won't. That's one PHP process. Attackers use graphics cards built for exactly this kind of arithmetic, and get billions of md5 guesses a second, which tears through every common password and most uncommon ones before lunch. sha1() and sha256 are just as fast, and just as wrong.

    Fix It

    The fix is one function, password_hash(), and its partner password_verify(). The fixed signup is public/index.php:

  • password_hash() is slow on purpose. With PASSWORD_DEFAULT, it's bcrypt at a cost of 12, PHP 8.4's default. A visitor waits a fraction of a second once, at signup; an attacker waits that long for every guess.
  • It salts every hash. Each one gets its own random salt, stored inside the hash, so two people with the same password get different hashes, and a table of precomputed hashes is useless.
  • A length rule, not a recipe. At least 12 characters, and nothing about capitals, digits or symbols. Rules like those mostly produce Password1!. Length is what makes guessing slow. The form suggests a few words in a row, which is long and easy to remember.
  • No more than 72 bytes. bcrypt only looks at the first 72 bytes of a password and silently ignores the rest. Rather than let that happen, the form says so. It counts bytes with strlen(), not characters, because that's what bcrypt counts: é is two.
  • A list of common passwords. The passwords attackers try first are no secret. Turn them down. A real site checks against a much longer list.
  • The database decides about duplicates. Checking "is this address taken?" with a SELECT first leaves a gap: two signups at the same moment both see "no." The UNIQUE index can't be raced, so the code just tries the INSERT and catches MySQL's error 1062. The collation makes CAROL@example.com the same address as carol@example.com.

    • Open http://localhost:8000 and sign up as carol@example.com with correct horse battery staple, then as dave@example.com with the same password. Look at the table again:

    carol@example.com $2y$12$... dave@example.com $2y$12$...

    Two long hashes, different from each other, for the same password. $2y$ says bcrypt, and 12$ says the cost. Point crack.php at one of those and it can't even start, and if it could, it would manage a handful of guesses a second. Never trust input still applies to the rest of the form: try password1234, or Carol's address in capitals, and see what you get.

    Gotchas
  • Never trim, change or limit the characters of a password. Spaces, accents, emoji: all fine, all part of it. The only cap is the 72 bytes bcrypt can use.
  • The column must hold more than 60 characters. bcrypt hashes are 60 today, but PASSWORD_DEFAULT can change in a future PHP, and its hashes may be longer. VARCHAR(255) is what the PHP manual recommends.
  • A signup form tells strangers who has an account. "That address already has an account" is a way to test addresses. The careful alternative is to always say "check your email," and send a message either way, but sending mail at signup has its own problem, below. Decide which you'd rather have, and rate limit the form either way.
  • Never send someone their password. Not in a welcome email, not in a reset. You can't, if you stored it properly, and that's the point.
    • One of my apps sent a verification email the moment anyone signed up. Someone noticed, and used the signup form to bury strangers' inboxes: sign up with their address, over and over, and our server sent each one an email every time. The fix was to send no email at signup at all, to put rate limits on every kind of mail the app sends, and to add a bot check.

    autocomplete="new-password" on a signup's password field tells password managers to offer a long random one and save it, and autocomplete="current-password" on a sign-in tells them to fill it in. Two attributes, and the people who use password managers end up with much better passwords than any rule could get out of them.

    The signup stores a good hash. Now someone has to type the password back in, and an attacker would like to type a lot of them. Next: a login form.
