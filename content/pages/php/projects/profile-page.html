Profile Page Phillip Harrington

Alice can sign in. Now she wants a profile: a display name and a few lines about herself, that she can change whenever she likes. The page checks that she's signed in, takes the form, and saves it. Everything you've learned says that's enough.

It isn't, because her browser will send her cookie with any form aimed at your site, including a form on someone else's page. That's cross-site request forgery, CSRF. You'll build the profile page naive, change Alice's profile from a second site she merely visits, then fix it with a token that only your own pages know.

    Break It

    Now be the other site. Make attacker/index.html, a page that promises a prize and contains a form aimed at your profile page, sent the moment it loads:

    In the same browser, still signed in to your site, visit http://localhost:8001. The page flashes "Claiming your prize..." and lands on your profile page, which now says:

    Hacked Buy cheap watches at https://spam.example

    Alice never touched your form. The other site's form posted to your page, and her browser, as it always does, attached her cookie for your site. The naive page saw a signed-in member posting a profile, and saved it. It can't tell the difference between a form on your page and a form on anyone's.

    Here it's a profile. The same trick works on any form that changes something: an email address, a shipping address, a "delete my account" button, a transfer. The attacker never sees the response; they don't need to.

    Why didn't SameSite=Lax stop it? Because localhost:8001 and localhost:8000 are the same site. Ports don't count, and on the real web, neither do subdomains: blog.example.com and example.com are one site, so a hole in one is a launch pad for the other. SameSite is a good second defense. It isn't the first.

    Fix It

    The fix is a secret that your pages put in every form, and that the other site can't know. src/csrf.php:

  • One token per session, 32 random bytes from random_bytes(), written as 64 hex characters, made the first time a form asks for it and kept in the session.
  • csrf_field() puts it in a form, as a hidden field. Your own pages can read the session; the attacker's page can't, and it can't read your page either, because the browser won't let one site read another's responses.
  • csrf_check() compares it with hash_equals(), and stops with a 403 if it's missing, wrong, or not a string at all.

    The token check is the first thing a POST does, before anything is read or changed. Then never trust input, as always: the name and bio are checked for type, encoding and length, and escaped when they're shown. Point the attacker's form at the fixed page, by changing naive-profile.php to profile.php in its action, and visit http://localhost:8001 again:

    That form had expired. Go back, reload the page, and try again.

    A 403, and Alice's profile is untouched. The other site sent everything but the token, because it had no way to know it. Your own form, with the token in it, saves as before.

    Gotchas
  • Every form that changes something needs the token. Saving a profile, changing an email, signing out, deleting a thing. Forget one, and that one is the hole. A site with many forms should check the token in one place every POST passes through.
  • GET must never change anything. The token protects forms. A link that deletes something can be triggered by an image tag on any page, token or not.
  • An XSS hole beats any token. A script running on your own page can read the token out of the form and post it. That's one more reason to escape everything, as in the Guestbook.
  • The browser tells you where a request came from, too. Modern browsers send Sec-Fetch-Site and Origin with form posts, and refusing posts that say cross-site is a good extra check. They're headers, which makes them input, so they back the token up and never replace it.
    • CSRF was also called "session riding" and "one-click attacks," which describes it well: the victim does nothing but visit a page while signed in somewhere else. It was on the OWASP Top Ten list of web application risks for years, until frameworks with tokens built in made it rare enough to drop off.

    Honest people hit the token check too: a form left open overnight in a tab whose session has expired. Make the 403 say what happened and what to do, as this one does, rather than a bare "Forbidden." It turns a mystery into a reload.

    The profile is safe from other sites. One change still needs more than a token, because it's the change that locks Alice out of her own account. Next: a change password form.
