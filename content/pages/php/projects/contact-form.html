Contact Form Phillip Harrington

Sending Email sent one message to an address you typed. A contact form does the same thing with an address a stranger typed, as many times as the stranger likes. That's the whole danger. A form that sends mail is a machine anyone on the internet can use to send mail from your server, and if you're careless about what it sends and to whom, it will be used for exactly that.

You'll build the friendly version first, with a "send me a copy" box, then use it to bury a stranger's inbox, then fix it so it can only ever do one thing: send you a message, at a pace a person would.

What You'll Use
  • Sending Email: Symfony Mailer and Mailpit.
  • Forms: fields, and checking them on the server.
  • Never Trust Input: every field, every time.
  • Rate Limiting: how many, how often, from whom.
  • Autoloading with Composer and PSR-4: a class of your own in src.

    Break It

    The copy goes to whatever address was typed. It doesn't have to be yours. From a second terminal, point the form at someone else, five times, with a message of your choosing:

    for i in 1 2 3 4 5; do curl -s -o /dev/null -d 'name=Prize&email=victim@example.com&copy=on&message=You won! https://spam.example' http://localhost:8000/naive.php done

    Mailpit now has ten new messages. Five went to the owner. The other five went to victim@example.com, who never visited your site, with "You won!" and a link, sent by your server. Make it five thousand, from a few hundred machines, and your server is a spam service, your domain's reputation is gone, and your real mail starts landing in spam folders too.

    Look at who the owner's copies claim to be from: victim@example.com. The form put the visitor's address in From, so your server is also forging mail, which is exactly what SPF and DMARC exist to catch.

    One more. Send an address that isn't one:

    curl -d 'name=x&email=nope&message=hi' http://localhost:8000/naive.php <b>Fatal error</b>: Uncaught Symfony\Component\Mime\Exception\RfcComplianceException: Email &quot;nope&quot; does not comply with addr-spec of RFC 2822. in /app/vendor/symfony/mime/Address.php:58 Stack trace: #0 /app/vendor/symfony/mime/Address.php(100): ...

    A crash, with your file paths and a stack trace, shown to whoever asked. Never trust input, not even to be the shape the form said it would be.

    The Fixed Form

    Then public/index.php:

  • Mail goes only to you. There's no copy box. A contact form sends one message, to one address, and that address is in your code, not in the request. That alone takes the cannon apart.
  • From you, not from them. From is the site's own address. The visitor goes in Reply-To, so pressing Reply still reaches them.
  • Every field is checked. field() insists on a string, valid UTF-8 and a length limit, and the address has to pass FILTER_VALIDATE_EMAIL before it goes near the mailer. Bad input gets a polite note and the form back, with what they typed escaped, never a stack trace.
  • A honeypot. The website field is hidden, so people never fill it in. Simple bots fill in every field they find. If it has anything in it, the form says "thanks" and sends nothing, so the bot learns nothing.
  • Two limits. Three messages per visitor every ten minutes, and twenty an hour for the whole site. The first stops one sender. The second stops a crowd of them, each staying under the first. Over either, the answer is 429 Too Many Requests with a Retry-After header.

    • Run the attack again, at the fixed form:

    for i in 1 2 3 4 5; do curl -s -o /dev/null -w '%{http_code}\n' -d 'name=Prize&email=victim@example.com&copy=on&message=You won!' http://localhost:8000/ done

    303, 303, 303, 429, 429. Three messages reach Mailpit, all to owner@example.com, from hello@example.com, with the visitor in Reply-To. The stranger gets nothing, ever. The bad address gets "An email address we can reply to, please."

    Gotchas
  • The limit is keyed on an address that may be your proxy's. Behind a CDN or load balancer, every visitor has the same REMOTE_ADDR, and three per ten minutes becomes three for the whole world. Use the address your own proxy reports, as in What's in the Middle.
  • The honeypot stops only simple bots. Anything that renders the page like a browser skips hidden fields. It's a cheap filter, not a wall. The limits are the wall; a bot check, like a CAPTCHA, is the next brick.
  • The message is still input when it reaches you. It's plain text here, so it can't run anything in your mail program. Send it as HTML and you'd have to escape it, or a stranger is writing HTML into your inbox.
  • Files fill up. One small file per visitor, forever. A real site keeps limits in something that expires keys by itself, like Redis, or cleans the folder on a schedule.
    • In 1978, Gary Thuerk, a marketer at Digital Equipment Corporation, sent one message announcing new computers to a few hundred people on the ARPANET, all at once. People complained, loudly. The name "spam" came years later, from a Monty Python sketch in which the word drowns out everything else.

    Put a hard daily cap on every kind of mail your site sends, and log a warning when you're near it. Real traffic grows slowly. A form being abused jumps from twenty a day to twenty thousand, and a cap turns that from a disaster into a log line you read over coffee.
