Translation Phillip Harrington

The log viewer only ever needed English. Most sites eventually need more. You'll build a small shop page that speaks English, French, Polish and Japanese: it picks a language from what the browser asks for, lets the visitor change it, pulls product names from the database in that language, and counts things the way each language counts.

Translating the words is the easy part. The work is everything around them: text that survives the trip from database to browser, a page that says which language it's in, and plurals, which English makes look much simpler than they are.

    Step 3: Picking a Language

    Browsers send the visitor's languages, in order of preference, in a header:

    Accept-Language: fr-CA,fr;q=0.9,en;q=0.5

    Canadian French first, any French next, English if nothing else. The q is a weight from 0 to 1; no q means 1. src/i18n.php reads it, and holds the other two pieces of the project as well:

    negotiateLanguage() splits the header, skips any part that isn't shaped like a language tag, sorts by weight, and takes the first one on our list, counting fr-CA as fr. Never trust input: a header is whatever the client typed, so the only languages that can come out are the four we put in.

    Step 4: Plural Rules

    pluralCategory() is the part English hides. English has two forms: one, and everything else, including zero. French uses the singular for zero too. Polish has three for whole numbers: 1 is one form, numbers ending in 2, 3 or 4 are another (except 12, 13 and 14), and the rest are a third. Japanese doesn't change at all. The category names, one, few, many and other, come from CLDR, the Unicode project that collects these rules for hundreds of languages.

    plurals.php prints the same message for a few numbers in each language:

    docker compose run --rm app php plurals.php en: 0 products | 1 product | 2 products | 5 products | 12 products | 22 products | 25 products fr: 0 produit | 1 produit | 2 produits | 5 produits | 12 produits | 22 produits | 25 produits pl: 0 produktów | 1 produkt | 2 produkty | 5 produktów | 12 produktów | 22 produkty | 25 produktów ja: 商品0点 | 商品1点 | 商品2点 | 商品5点 | 商品12点 | 商品22点 | 商品25点

    Look at Polish: 2 and 22 match, 5 and 12 match, and no rule about "more than one" gets you there.

    Step 5: The Page

    public/index.php puts it together:

  • The language comes from a ?lang= link first, then the cookie that link sets, then Accept-Language, then English. A visitor's own choice beats their browser's guess. All three are input, and all three are checked against the list.
  • Product names come from one query: join the English row always, the visitor's language if there is one, and COALESCE() takes the first that isn't NULL.
  • The headers say UTF-8 (the last boundary), name the language with Content-Language, and send Vary: Accept-Language, Cookie, which tells every cache in the middle that this URL has more than one answer.
  • The HTML says <html lang="fr">, so screen readers pronounce it in French and browsers hyphenate and pick fonts correctly. Each language's name in the switcher is written in that language, and carries its own lang: "Français" is a French word even on the English page.
    • Step 6: Run It docker compose up

    Open http://localhost:8000 and switch languages with the links at the bottom. Reload, and the choice sticks. Then, from a second terminal, be a few different browsers:

    curl -H 'Accept-Language: fr-CA,fr;q=0.9,en;q=0.5' http://localhost:8000 curl -H 'Accept-Language: en;q=0.2, pl;q=0.8' http://localhost:8000 curl -H 'Accept-Language: ja' http://localhost:8000 curl -H 'Accept-Language: de' http://localhost:8000

    -H, as before, adds a header to the request: here, the languages a browser would ask for.

    Programmers abbreviate "internationalization" as i18n: the first letter, the last letter, and the 18 letters in between. "Localization" is l10n the same way. Internationalization is making the code able to speak any language; localization is the work of making it speak a particular one. Gotchas
  • One boundary not in UTF-8 breaks them all. A table in latin1, a connection without charset=utf8mb4, a page without a charset: any one is enough. When text arrives garbled, check each boundary in order.
  • Don't pick the language from the address. Guessing from where a visitor seems to be is wrong for travelers, for people who live in one country and read another language, and for every country with more than one language. Accept-Language is what the person actually set.
  • Strings get longer. German and French often run noticeably longer than English, sometimes by a third or more. Leave room in buttons and menus, and don't cut text to a fixed length with substr(), which can split a character in half; use mb_substr().
  • These plural rules are simplified. They're right for whole numbers in these four languages. Decimals have rules of their own, and some languages have six forms. For real work, PHP's intl extension has MessageFormatter, which knows every CLDR rule, and Locale::acceptFromHttp() for the header. It isn't in the php:8.4-cli image; you add it in the Dockerfile, with the ICU library it needs.
  • Without Vary, a cache picks one language for everyone. The first visitor's French page gets kept and handed to the next visitor, who asked for Japanese. A separate URL per language, like /fr/, avoids the problem altogether, and search engines prefer it.

    • Before any real translations exist, make a fake one: every English string wrapped in brackets, padded by a third, with an accent or two, like [Wélcome!!!!]. Switch to it and read every page. Anything still in plain English was never run through a translation, and anything cut off or overflowing won't fit in German either.
