Shopping Cart Phillip Harrington

The store front shows what's for sale. A cart remembers what someone wants while they keep looking: add a mug, change it to two, take out the shirt. It lives in the session, from one page to the next, until they check out or wander off.

The one rule that matters: the cart holds product ids and quantities, and nothing else. Not names, and above all not prices. Every time the cart is shown, the prices come from the database. A cart that trusts a price someone sent is a shop that sells at whatever price the customer likes.

    •  Build It Step 1: The Setup

    The containers, Dockerfile and init.sql are the ones from Store Front, with the database called shop. This time there's a composer.json, for the autoloader and for PHPUnit, which is only needed while developing, so it goes in require-dev:

    docker run --rm -v "$PWD":/app -w /app composer:2 composer install

    src/Database.php is the connection class from Queues, in the Shop namespace.

    Step 2: The Cart

    src/Cart.php knows the rules and nothing about the web: whole numbers only, zero removes a line, never more than 99 of a thing or 50 lines in all.

    The constructor rebuilds a cart from whatever was saved in the session, and drops anything that isn't an integer id and an integer quantity. The session is stored on your server, but what went into it came from requests. Treat it as input that has been through one check already, not as proof.

    Step 3: Test It

    Because Cart doesn't touch the database or the session, it can be tested on its own, in a few milliseconds. tests/CartTest.php:

    docker compose run --rm app vendor/bin/phpunit tests

    OK (6 tests, ...). Negative quantities, a million mugs, junk in the session: each is a rule, and each rule has a test that says so.

    Step 6: Run It docker compose up

    Open http://localhost:8000. Add two black mugs, then a big mug, then open the cart. Change a quantity to 0 and press Update, and the line goes. Try to add 50 big mugs: the cart says only 8 left and charges for 8.

    Now be the customer who wants a discount. Get a session and its token, add a mug with a price of your own, and look at the cart:

    curl -s -c jar http://localhost:8000 | grep -o 'name="csrf" value="[a-f0-9]*"' | head -1 curl -b jar -d csrf=PASTE_TOKEN -d action=add -d product=1 -d price_cents=1 http://localhost:8000/cart.php curl -b jar http://localhost:8000/cart.php

    What's new:

    Print only the part of each line that matches, not the whole line: here, the hidden form field with the token in it. Keep just the first line of that. A placeholder: put the token from the first command here.

    The mug is in at $14.00. Never trust input: the cart reads the three fields it needs, checks each one, and never even looks at the rest.

    The wheeled shopping cart came long before the online one. Sylvan Goldman, who owned a chain of grocery stores in Oklahoma City, introduced it in 1937: a folding chair with wheels and two baskets. Shoppers were reluctant at first, and he reportedly hired people to push carts around his stores until customers followed. The web borrowed the name, and the little icon, almost as soon as there were web shops. Gotchas
  • A price in a hidden field is a suggestion. Any value the browser sends can be changed. Prices, discounts, totals and shipping are all computed on the server, from your own data, every time.
  • Prices change while carts wait. The cart shows today's price because it never stored one. When the order is placed, that's when the price gets copied and fixed. That's Checkout.
  • Stock in the cart isn't stock reserved. Two people can each have the last mug in their cart. Check stock again when the order is placed, and decide then who gets it.
  • Sessions expire. A cart in the session vanishes when the session does. Logged-in customers expect a cart to follow them between devices; that means a carts table, keyed to the user.
  • Every cart-changing form needs the token. Without it, another site could fill a visitor's cart, or empty it, with a form that posts to yours.

    • Pull the rules out of the page into a class that takes plain values and returns plain values, like Cart. Then a test is three lines and runs in a millisecond, and nobody has to click through the shop to find out whether -5 breaks the total.
