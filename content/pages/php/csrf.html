CSRF Phillip Harrington

XSS needed the attacker's code on your page. CSRF doesn't. It works from a page on a completely different site, which quietly makes your visitor's browser send a request to yours. The browser adds your cookies, as it always does, and your site sees a logged-in member asking to change their email address.

What's CSRF?

Cross-site request forgery. A forged request: one that carries a real member's login cookie, sent by their own browser, that they never meant to send. Any page can contain a form aimed at any site, and a script can submit it the moment the page loads. The browser sends the target site's cookies with it, because that's how cookies work: they go with every request to their site, no matter which page started it.

So a session cookie proves which browser sent a request. It doesn't prove the person meant to send it, or that it came from your page. A login cookie is input too, and you never trust input to say more than it can.

What's a CSRF Token?

A long random secret, stored in the member's session and put in a hidden field in every form your site draws. When a form comes back, the server checks the field against the session. Another site can make the browser send the cookie, but it can't read your pages, so it can't know the token. Without the token, the request is refused.

Site and Origin

Two words browsers use precisely. An origin is the scheme, host and port together: http://localhost:8000 and http://localhost:8001 are different origins. A site is looser: the scheme and the registered domain, ignoring the port and any subdomains. shop.example.com and blog.example.com are the same site. That difference matters below.

How It Works
  1. Alice logs in to your site. Her browser keeps the session cookie.
  2. Later, in another tab, she opens a page on the attacker's site. It has a hidden form aimed at your "change email" page, with the attacker's address filled in.
  3. A script on that page submits the form as soon as it loads.
  4. Alice's browser sends the POST to your site, and adds your session cookie to it.
  5. Your code sees a POST from a logged-in member and changes her email address to the attacker's. Next, the attacker asks for a password reset, and the email comes to them.

With a token, step five changes: there's no csrf field in the forged form, or a wrong one, and your code refuses it. Alice's real form, drawn by your page, has the right one.

In 1988, Norm Hardy published a short paper about a compiler at Tymshare, a time-sharing company. The compiler was allowed to write to the company's billing file, so it could record usage. A user told it to write its output to a file name of their choosing, and chose the billing file. The compiler used its own permission on the user's behalf, and the billing records were gone. Hardy called it the confused deputy. CSRF is the same problem: your site acts on the cookie's authority, for a request somebody else chose. Try It

In PHP
  • bin2hex(random_bytes(32)) makes the token: 32 bytes from the operating system's secure random source, as 64 hex characters. Never rand(), uniqid() or md5(time()), which can be guessed.
  • Keep it in $_SESSION, and print it into every form that changes something.
  • hash_equals($_SESSION['csrf'], $token) to compare. It takes the same time whether the first character is wrong or the last, so the timing gives nothing away.
  • Check that the token is_string() first: csrf[]=x makes it an array, and hash_equals() would throw.
  • http_response_code(403) when it's missing or wrong, and change nothing.
  • session_set_cookie_params(['samesite' => 'Lax']) as a second layer. Sessions and Cookie Flags has the rest of the cookie's settings.
    • Gotchas
  • A GET must never change anything. An <img src> on any page makes a GET to any address, with cookies. If /delete?id=7 deletes, an image tag deletes. Changes are POSTs, and POSTs need the token.
  • SameSite doesn't stop the same site. SameSite=Lax keeps cookies off POSTs from other sites, and it's worth setting. But a page on another subdomain, or another port, is the same site, and the cookie goes. Try It is exactly that case. The token works either way.
  • Don't put the token in a URL. URLs end up in logs, in history, and in the Referer header sent to other sites. Hidden field for forms, a request header for fetch().
  • XSS beats the token. A script running on your own page can read the token and send it. CSRF protection assumes you've fixed XSS first.
  • Login forms need one too. A forged login signs the visitor in to the attacker's account, and anything they then type, like a card or an address, goes into it.

    Give the session a token, put it in the form, and refuse any POST that doesn't bring it back. Make site/index.php:

    And evil/fixed.html, the same attack aimed at the fixed page. It's evil/index.html with one change, the form's address:

    <form id="prize" method="post" action="http://localhost:8000/">

    Open http://localhost:8000/, then http://localhost:8001/fixed.html. This time you land on "That form didn't come from this site." with a 403, and your email hasn't changed. The forged form had no token. Your own form, on your own page, still works, because your page put the token in it. Ctrl+C stops both sites.

    Don't check the token in each page. Check it once, at the top of every POST, before any page code runs: in a shared include today, in middleware later. Then a new form is protected the day you write it, and forgetting isn't possible. Pages that truly need no token, like a webhook from a payment company, get an explicit exception you can list.
