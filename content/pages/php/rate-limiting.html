Rate Limiting Phillip Harrington

Sessions and Cookie Flags protected the login once someone has it. But a login form, out of the box, answers every guess as happily as the first, as fast as anyone can send them. Every password is guessable given enough tries. A rate limit decides how many tries "enough" is.

What's a Rate Limit?

A cap on how often something can happen in a stretch of time: ten failed logins per account in fifteen minutes, five password reset emails per address per hour, a hundred API requests per key per minute. Under the cap, everything works. Over it, the server stops doing the work and says so.

What to Count By
  • The account, like the email address being logged in to. It stops many guesses at one person's password, from anywhere.
  • The IP address the request came from. It stops one machine guessing at many accounts.
  • Both, which is the usual answer, with a higher limit for the address, since a whole office or school can share one.
    • What's a 429?

    429 Too Many Requests, the status code for "slow down." It can come with a Retry-After header saying how many seconds to wait. See Request and Response for the rest of the codes.

    How It Works
    1. A login arrives, with an email address, a password, and an IP address.
    2. Before checking the password, count the failures for that email, and for that address, in the last fifteen minutes.
    3. If either count is at its limit, answer 429 and stop. Don't check the password at all, so a locked-out guess learns nothing, not even whether it was right.
    4. Otherwise, check the password. If it's wrong, record a failure: the email, the address, the time. If it's right, clear that email's failures and log them in.
    5. Old failures fall out of the window on their own. After fifteen quiet minutes, the count is back to zero.

    Ten guesses every fifteen minutes is under a thousand a day. The hundred-word list that takes eight seconds in Break It would take ten hours against one account, and a list of a million would take years. The per-address limit makes spreading out across accounts slow too.

    In PHP
  • SELECT COUNT(*) ... WHERE `email` = ? AND `failed_at` > NOW() - INTERVAL 15 MINUTE, with an index that starts with the column you count by.
  • http_response_code(429) and header('Retry-After: 900') when someone's over the limit.
  • $_SERVER['REMOTE_ADDR'] for the address, when PHP faces visitors directly. Behind a proxy or CDN, take the entry your own layer added to X-Forwarded-For, as in What's in the Middle.
  • VARCHAR(45) holds any IPv4 or IPv6 address as text.
    •  Gotchas
  • Behind a proxy, everyone has the same address. Count by REMOTE_ADDR behind a CDN, and fifty failures from anyone locks out everyone. Count by the first X-Forwarded-For entry, and anyone can claim a new address with every guess. Never trust input; headers are input.
  • A per-account limit is also a way to lock someone out. Anyone can send ten wrong passwords for Alice and keep her out for fifteen minutes. Keep the window short, let it expire on its own, never lock an account for good, and consider a bot check after a few failures instead of a hard stop.
  • Attackers spread out. Credential stuffing tries passwords leaked from other sites, one or two per account, across thousands of accounts and addresses. Limits slow it down; checking new passwords against leaks and offering a second factor do more.
  • Parallel requests can slip past. Twenty guesses sent at the same instant can all count zero failures before any of them records one. For a login, a few extra guesses rarely matter. For something like redeeming a code, lock the row or count in a transaction.
  • The same answer, the same time. "Wrong email or password" for both cases, as here. But looking up a missing email is faster than checking a bcrypt hash, and the difference can be measured. Careful logins verify against a dummy hash when the email doesn't exist.
  • Logins aren't the only thing. Anything that sends an email or a text message, costs you money, or is slow, needs a limit: signups, password resets, contact forms, search.
    • One of my apps had a signup form that sent a verification email straight away. Someone found it and started signing up strangers' addresses, so each stranger got an email they never asked for, from my app. The form had become a way to flood other people's inboxes. The fix was three things: no email at signup at all, rate limits on every email the app sends, and a bot check on the form. Break It

    Forty-three wrong answers and one right one, in about eight seconds. Nothing stopped it, nothing slowed it, and nothing anywhere noticed. With a bigger list and a few hours, almost any account with a weak password falls the same way.

    Ten 401s, then a 429 for everything else, including "tigger." The right password was in the list, and it didn't matter: once the account was over its limit, the page stopped checking. The loop runs to the end of the list without getting in. Ask with curl -i to see the Retry-After: 900. To start over, docker compose down -v deletes the database, failures and all.

    One of my apps got its login limit after the fact, not at launch: 10 failures per email address and 50 per IP, in 15 minutes, the same numbers as this lesson. Until then, its login was the naive one in Break It. That's the trouble with guessing: when it works, it looks like any other login.

    Write a line to your log every time a limit trips: what was limited, by which key, and the count. Most days there are none. When there are suddenly hundreds, someone is trying, and you find out from your own log instead of from a member whose account was taken.
