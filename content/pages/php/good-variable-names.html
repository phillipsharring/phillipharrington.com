Good Variable Names Phillip Harrington

A coding standard decides where the spaces go. It can't decide what to call things, and names do more for a reader than any layout. A good name answers the question the reader was about to ask. A bad one sends them back up the file to work it out.

What's a Good Name?

One that says what the thing holds, in words the reader already uses.

  • What, not what type. $emails, not $arr. $renewsOn, not $date. The type is already in the code; the meaning is only in the name.
  • Units in the name. $timeoutSeconds, $priceCents, $maxUploadBytes. Half the bugs with numbers are someone reading seconds as milliseconds.
  • Booleans read as yes-or-no. $isActive, $hasPaid, $autoRenews, so if ($hasPaid) reads like a sentence.
  • Plural for many, singular for one. foreach ($subscriptions as $subscription) tells you which is the list.
  • Length to match the reach. $i in a three-line loop is fine. A variable that lives for fifty lines needs a real name.
  • One word per idea. If it's "customer" in one place, don't make it "client" in the next. A reader assumes two words mean two things.
    • NASA lost the Mars Climate Orbiter in 1999 because of units. Software on the ground reported thruster impulse in pound-force seconds, and the navigation software that used the numbers expected newton-seconds. The spacecraft came in far too low and was lost. A number without its unit is half a number. Names to Stop Using

    $data, $info, $tmp, $temp, $result for anything that lives longer than a line or two, $obj, $arr, $x, $flag, and anything with a number on the end ($user2). Each one is a name that could mean anything, so it means nothing.

    Early FORTRAN picked a variable's type from its first letter: names starting with I through N were integers, and everything else was a floating-point number. Programmers turned it into a joke, "God is real, unless declared integer." Names carrying meaning the reader has to know is an old problem. How It Works

    When you read a line, you hold every name in it in your head at once:

    1. You meet a name.
    2. If it says what it is, you move on.
    3. If it doesn't, you go and find where it was set, read that, and come back, holding the answer while you read the rest.

    Three or four of those in one condition, and the reader is out of room. Good names keep everyone at step two.

    In PHP
  • Variables are $camelCase in most PHP code today. Some projects use $snake_case. Pick the project's, and stick to it.
  • Variable names are case-sensitive: $user and $User are two variables. Function and class names aren't, which is one more reason not to lean on case.
  • Array keys are names too. $subscription['renewsOn'] beats $x[1], and a class with typed properties beats both, once the shape is used in more than one place (see Classes and Objects).
  • extract() turns an array's keys into variables. Never use it on input, and there's rarely a reason to use it at all.
    • Gotchas
  • Never let input name your variables. That's the Try It's extract.php: one query string, and you're an administrator. Never trust input, including the names in it. PHP used to do this to every script, with a setting called register_globals; it was turned off by default in PHP 4.2 and removed in PHP 5.4.
  • A name that lies is worse than a bad one. If $activeUsers starts holding every user, rename it. Readers believe names.
  • Abbreviations save typing once and cost reading forever. $custAddrLn2 is quicker to type than $customerAddressLine2, and every reader pays for it after. Your editor will finish the long one for you.
  • Don't rename by find-and-replace. Replacing $user also hits $username and $users. Use your editor's rename, which knows the difference.
    • "There are only two hard things in Computer Science: cache invalidation and naming things." It's usually credited to Phil Karlton, a programmer at Netscape. People have been adding a third for years: off-by-one errors.

    Search a big project for $id and you get thousands of hits. Search for $invoiceId and you get the ones you meant. A name specific enough to find with a search is usually specific enough to read.
